The BTW sandpile

One grain can do nothing at all, or set off an avalanche across the grid. What changes if the rule is always the same?

Bak · Tang · Wiesenfeld

A small model of self-organised criticality: how a system can develop cascades at many scales without tuning an external parameter to a threshold.

03 / Local rules

Four grains and an open edge

Each site holds an integer height h. Add one grain, then wait until the grid is stable before adding another. When h reaches 4, the site topples: it loses four grains and sends one in each cardinal direction.

hsite → hsite − 4;   hneighbour → hneighbour + 1

Grains sent off-grid are lost. Away from the edge, toppling conserves grains. The order of legal topplings changes neither the final heights nor the total number of topplings. That’s the model’s Abelian property.

04 / Reading the chart

What a power law would look like

If positive avalanche sizes obeyed a power law, their probability mass would fall as s−τ. For τ > 1, the large-s tail would then scale as:

P(S ≥ s | S > 0) ∝ s1−τ

That would look straight over the scaling range on log–log axes. A short straight stretch isn’t proof. Finite grids limit the tail, and the two-dimensional BTW model has more complicated scaling than one universal fitted line would suggest.

05 / Why it matters

Simple rules, no typical cascade size

Slow input builds up the pile; local topplings spread activity; the edges let grains escape. After a long run, the grid can produce cascades over many scales even though every input is the same size. Broad scaling here means no single typical scale within the scaling range, not that a finite board can produce arbitrarily large events.

06 / Beyond the grid

A possibility proof, not a diagnosis

Think of a fault that accumulates stress slowly and releases it in slips of very different sizes. The sandpile shows how local thresholds and redistribution can generate that kind of broad spread. It doesn’t establish the mechanism behind real earthquakes. Different processes can give similar-looking plots, so field data and independent evidence still matter.
