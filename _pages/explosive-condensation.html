What is explosive condensation?

Imagine a ring of boxes, each holding some number of balls. At each tick, you pick one ball at random and move it to a neighbouring box. If the move is completely unbiased, every box ends up with roughly the same number of balls. That is normal diffusive mixing.

Now add a twist: the probability of a ball moving to a box depends on how many balls are already there. The richer the box, the more likely it is to attract new arrivals. This is the misanthrope process with nonlinear jump rates.

With strong enough nonlinearity, a condensate appears — one box hoovers up a finite fraction of all the balls. In the explosive regime, this condensate forms so fast that the time to reach the steady state actually vanishes as the system grows. It is the spatial analogue of "instantaneous gelation" in mean-field coagulation.

The model in detail

The model is a one-dimensional lattice of L sites with periodic boundary conditions (a ring), each site i holding an integer occupation ni ≥ 0. The total number of particles is conserved:

Σi=1L ni = M (fixed)

Time is continuous. A particle at site i hops to the left or right neighbour with rate u(ni, ni±1). The function u(n,m) is the jump rate from a site with n particles to a neighbour with m particles.

Factorized steady state

A remarkable property of this class of models is that they admit a factorized steady state. The probability of finding the system in configuration {n1, …, nL} is:

P(n1, …, nL) = (1/ZL,M) · ∏i=1L f(ni)

where f(n) is the single-site weight and ZL,M is the normalization (canonical partition function). This factorization makes the model analytically tractable — it is one of the few spatially-extended non-equilibrium systems where the full steady state is known exactly.

The specific jump rate

In our paper we studied a family of models where the jump rate depends on the target site's occupation via a power law:

u(n, m) ∝ mγ

(up to a mass-dependent factor that ensures the factorization property holds). The exponent γ controls the nonlinearity:

  • γ = 0: unbiased random walk. No condensation.
  • 0 < γ < 1: weak attraction. Condensation occurs, but via normal coarsening — the relaxation time grows with system size.
  • γ ≥ 1: strong attraction. Explosive condensation — the condensate forms in vanishing time as L → ∞.

The demo above uses β = γ for the slider, so set β ≥ 1 to see the explosive regime.

Results

What Waclaw & Evans proved (2012)

Barłomiej Waclaw and Martin Evans showed that in the asymmetric case — where particles can only hop in one direction (say, clockwise) — the model exhibits explosive condensation when γ > 1. The mechanism is simple: once a condensate forms, it grows by absorbing particles from upstream faster than diffusion can redistribute them. The time to stationarity goes to zero as the system size increases.

Our contribution (2015)

The key question we asked was: does explosion survive if particles can hop in both directions? Symmetric dynamics are physically more realistic — they occur in isotropic systems like granular gases and colloids — but they also allow the condensate to lose particles by back-diffusion, which naively might seem to suppress the instability.

Our main results were:

  • Explosive condensation in 1D with symmetric hops does occur, provided γ ≥ 1. The critical exponent is the same threshold as in the asymmetric case.
  • Sub-threshold coarsening for 0 < γ < 1: the system still condenses, but the time to reach the steady state diverges with system size as a power law. This is ordinary coarsening, analogous to Ostwald ripening or domain growth in magnets.
  • Higher dimensions (d ≥ 2): we argued via mean-field reasoning that explosive condensation should be generic for all γ > 0, because the higher connectivity makes it impossible for back-diffusion to counteract the attraction.

The mechanism

The explosive regime works because of a positive feedback loop. Consider a small fluctuation where one site temporarily has more particles than its neighbours. With γ ≥ 1, the probability of a particle jumping into this site grows at least linearly with its excess mass, while the probability of a particle jumping out is independent of the site's mass (in the zero-range limit). The imbalance is self-reinforcing: the site gets richer, which makes it even more attractive, which makes it richer still. The runaway happens on a timescale that is a decreasing function of system size — hence "explosive."

In the symmetric case, the condensate can also move. It performs a biased random walk around the ring, absorbing smaller clusters it encounters. In the simulation above, this is what you see: a white-hot condensate sweeping the lattice and mopping up everything in its path.

Why this matters

Condensation in mass-transport models is a paradigm for phase separation in driven, non-equilibrium systems. It appears in:

  • Granular matter: When shaken, granular particles cluster; the inelastic collisions create an effective attractive dynamics.
  • Network traffic: Packets or vehicles on a network can pile up at bottlenecks, an analogy to condensation at a single lattice site.
  • Coagulation / aggregation: The mean-field limit of the model maps to the Smoluchowski coagulation equation, where the "explosive" regime corresponds to instantaneous gelation — a finite-size giant cluster appearing at t = 0⁺.
  • Active matter: Self-propelled particles with quorum sensing or alignment rules can show similar runaway clustering when local density enhances recruitment.

The distinction between normal coarsening (diverging time) and explosive condensation (vanishing time) is sharp: it separates systems that you can watch equilibrate from systems where the condensate appears essentially instantaneously on large scales. Our result extended the explosion from a special one-directional model to the more realistic symmetric case, making the physics applicable to a broader class of real systems.

About this demo

Sites are picked uniformly from the non-empty ones and each emits one particle per unit time. The destination is chosen with probability proportional to mβ. To make the late-stage coarsening visible in real time, a site with ≥15 particles is treated as a cluster: it can move collectively (the whole mass hops as a unit) or break up (shedding a small shard). This is exactly the fast-coarsening approximation I used years ago in the original simulations.
