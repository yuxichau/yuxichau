Interacting particle systems

Interacting particle systems form a branch of probability theory and statistical mechanics that studies large collections of random walkers whose movement rules depend on the state of their neighbours. The subject took shape in the 1970s through the work of Spitzer and Liggett, who gave rigorous mathematical structure to models that physicists had been simulating for years. The basic setup is always the same:

  • a lattice — here a one-dimensional ring of L sites with periodic boundaries;
  • an integer occupation number ni ≥ 0 on each site, evolving in continuous time as a Markov process;
  • stochastic jump rules — each site emits particles at random times, with a rate that is a function of the local configuration.

What makes these systems interesting is that the microscopic rules are completely local and memoryless, yet the macroscopic behaviour can be dramatic: particles can phase-separate into a dense condensate plus a dilute background, coarsen slowly like droplets of morning mist, or — in the case studied here — condense explosively, faster than any diffuse system of comparable size.

The most famous example is the zero-range process (ZRP), where a particle leaves site i at a rate depending only on ni, the occupation of the departure site itself. A close cousin is the misanthrope process, where the hop rate depends on both the departure and the destination site. These models matter because they are among the very few spatially extended non-equilibrium systems whose steady states are known exactly, so they serve as theoretical laboratories: you can compute precisely what "condensation" means, when it happens, and how fast, and then check the predictions against simulation.

The model in this paper is a misanthrope process with a destination-biased jump rate — the richer the target site, the faster particles pour into it. That single twist, taken far enough, produces explosive condensation.

The model

The lattice is a ring of L sites; site i holds ni particles, and the total is conserved:

Σi=1L ni = M (fixed)

A particle at site i hops to a neighbouring site j = i ± 1 with rate u(ni, nj). The family we studied takes the form

u(n, m) = v(n) · mγ

where v(n) is a mass-dependent prefactor chosen so the model keeps its exact solvability, and the interesting physics sits in the mγ factor: the jump rate grows as a power of the occupation of the destination site. The exponent γ tunes the attraction:

  • γ = 0 — plain diffusion. Particles spread out; no condensation.
  • 0 < γ < 1 — weak attraction. A condensate forms, but slowly: the relaxation time grows with system size (ordinary coarsening).
  • γ ≥ 1 — strong attraction. Explosive condensation: the time to reach the steady state vanishes as L → ∞.

Why the steady state is known exactly

This class of models admits a factorized steady state: the probability of a configuration splits into a product of single-site weights,

P(n1, …, nL) = (1/ZL,M) · ∏i=1L f(ni)

with f(n) the single-site weight and ZL,M the canonical partition function. For the power-law family above, f(n) inherits a boundary condition — for γ ≥ 1 the weight of a large occupation behaves like f(n) ~ n−γ, which fails to be normalisable in the grand-canonical ensemble above a critical density. That failure is the condensation transition: above density ρc, the excess mass has nowhere to go in the product measure and collapses onto a single site.

The factorization turns questions about condensation into questions about the large-n tail of one function f. It is the reason this model family is such a useful testing ground — and it is also why the dynamical questions (how fast does the condensate form?) remain hard and interesting: the stationary state says nothing directly about the path taken to reach it.

The original asymmetric result

The story begins with Waclaw & Evans, Phys. Rev. Lett. 108, 070601 (2012). They studied the model with asymmetric dynamics: every hop is to the right (say, clockwise), never to the left. The jump rate of a particle from site i into site i+1 is u(m) ∝ mγ, set by the occupation of the destination.

Their striking finding: for γ > 1, the system exhibits explosive condensation. Let T(L) denote the time for the system to reach its stationary state, starting from a homogeneous initial condition. In ordinary (non-explosive) condensation, T(L) grows with L — bigger systems take longer. Waclaw and Evans found that for γ > 1 the opposite happens: T(L) shrinks, apparently to zero, as L increases. The condensate forms faster and faster the bigger the system — a spatially extended version of the instantaneous gelation known from mean-field coagulation, where a giant cluster appears at a finite time (possibly t = 0+) in the thermodynamic limit.

The mechanism is a runaway positive feedback. Once a fluctuation gives one site extra mass, every site immediately upstream begins emitting into it at rate ∝ mγ with γ > 1 — faster than linear in the excess. The heavy site therefore attracts mass faster than one-for-one, grows, and attracts still faster. Because the dynamics are one-directional, there is no back-diffusion to oppose the inflow: nothing upstream can pull that mass back out. The instability is unopposed, and the bigger the lattice, the more mass is within reach of the growing condensate — hence the acceleration with system size.

One caveat they noted, and which motivated the follow-up work: in the asymmetric case the stationary state is not reached by a condensate sitting still. The condensate emits downstream and is fed from upstream, and it performs a jerky drift around the ring. Whether "time to stationarity" is even well-defined, and how to measure it cleanly, required care in the simulations.

Our result: explosion survives symmetry

Our paper asked the natural next question: does explosive condensation survive if particles can hop both ways? Symmetry matters physically — real systems (colloids, granular gases, ﬂuids) are isotropic — and it matters mathematically, because now the condensate has an escape route: back-diffusion. A site next to the condensate can, in principle, drain it. Intuition suggested that this leakage might tame the instability and restore ordinary coarsening.

It does not — provided the nonlinearity is strong enough. Our findings:

  • Symmetric 1D, γ ≥ 1: explosive condensation persists. The threshold is the same γ ≥ 1 as in the asymmetric case. The condensate forms in a time that does not grow with system size.
  • Symmetric 1D, 0 < γ < 1: condensation still occurs (above the critical density) but by ordinary coarsening — clusters merge slowly, and the equilibration time diverges with L as a power law, in the same universality class as droplet growth (Ostwald ripening).
  • Dimensions d ≥ 2: mean-field reasoning suggests explosive condensation becomes generic for all γ > 0 — with more neighbours feeding each site, the inflow advantage of a heavy site can no longer be balanced by back-diffusion at any positive γ.

The mechanism in the symmetric case is subtler than in the asymmetric one. Back-diffusion does drain the condensate — but it drains it isotropically and diffusively, as a slow leak ∝ ∂m, while the attraction fills it super-linearly ∝ mγ. For γ ≥ 1 the super-linear inflow always outruns the linear leak at large m, so the fixed point at "all mass in one site" is approached in finite, size-independent time. In the simulation below you can watch exactly this: a large cluster sweeps the ring, and its own diffusion is what carries it into fresh territory to absorb.

Why the symmetric case is much harder to simulate

The asymmetric model is, computationally, a friendly system. Every site has exactly one possible move (emit right), the total event rate is easy to bound, and the condensate advances by a deterministic drift. The symmetric model is a different animal, and the difficulty is not cosmetic — it changes what is measurable:

  • Two directions, weighted choices. Every emitting site now has two candidate destinations with rates mleftγ and mrightγ. Event bookkeeping roughly doubles, but that is the least of the problems.
  • The clock runs at the speed of the condensate. Simulations use event-driven (Gillespie) time: between events, the clock advances by Δt ~ Exp(1/Γ), where Γ = Σi ui is the total event rate. With destination-biased rates, the sites adjacent to a condensate of mass m* emit into it at rate ∝ (m*)γ. So as the condensate grows, Γ explodes, and each individual event advances physical time by only Δt ∝ (m*)−γ — a vanishingly small step.
  • Progress is measured in rare events. Reaching stationarity requires the condensate to make of order L net moves around the ring (to sweep up the background), and in the exact single-particle dynamics each one-site shift of the condensate requires O(m*) elementary hops. Total events: O(L · m*). With m* ∝ ρL, that is O(L²) events — each worth only Δt ∝ (m*)−γ of physical time. For lattices of 104–105 sites, generating a single equilibration run, let alone the ensemble needed to average T(L), took very long runs — precisely because the interesting quantity, the time to condensation, had to be measured against a clock whose ticks became smaller the closer the system got to the answer.

In short: the symmetric dynamics couple a super-linearly growing event rate to a sub-linearly growing physical progress rate. The simulation spends nearly all of its effort re-shuffling particles in and out of a condensate that barely moves. That is where the approximation comes in.

The large-cluster approximation

To make the symmetric dynamics tractable, I used a coarse-graining approximation for large clusters — the same idea implemented in the interactive demo on this page. Mathematically it is a time-rescaling of the collective degrees of freedom:

Setup

Let the condensate be a site with mass m ≥ mc (a coarse-graining threshold, mc = 15 in the demo). Separate the state into the condensate position Xt ∈ {1,…,L} and the residual background masses. In the exact dynamics, the centre of mass of the condensate shifts by one lattice site only after a sequence of single-particle hops — each hop transfers δ = 1 particle, and about m of them must accumulate to displace mass m by one site.

The approximation

Replace the m elementary single-particle transfers by a single collective transfer:

{m hops of δ = 1 at rate u}   ⟶   {1 hop of δ = m at rate u/m}

That is, when the condensate moves, its entire mass m jumps to the adjacent site as one object, with per-event probability p(mp(m) = 0.70 + 0.25 tanh((m − mc)/15), so large clusters hop almost every attempt). The one-site displacement of the centre of mass is preserved exactly; only the microscopic path taken to get there is integrated out.

Two details keep the approximation honest:

  • A small breakup probability ε. With probability ε (2% in the demo) a collective event instead sheds a small shard of 1–5 particles to the neighbour. This mimics the fluctuation-driven evaporation of the exact process, keeps the condensate from freezing into an absorbing state, and preserves ergodicity — the cluster can still lose its top position, as it does in the exact dynamics.
  • Correct mass-dependence of mobility. In the exact dynamics the diffusion constant of a cluster of mass m scales like D(m) ∝ 1/m — heavy clusters are slower per particle but their centre of mass still random-walks. The collective hop reproduces this one-site-per-event random walk of the centre of mass directly, rather than reconstructing it from m correlated single-particle hops.

Why it works

The justification is a scale separation. The internal redistribution of mass between two adjacent sites (who is "on top") equilibrates in O(1) event time, while the relocation of the centre of mass takes O(m) events. Since the coarsening dynamics depend on where the condensate is, and only weakly on its internal micro-state, integrating out the fast variable and boosting each relocation to a single event preserves the coarse-grained path measure — the sequence of condensate positions — at a fraction of the computational cost.

What it buys

  • Event count per condensate site-move: O(m*) → O(1). Over a full coarsening sweep: O(L·m*) → O(L). For L = 104 and m* ~ 103, that is a 103-fold reduction — the difference between a run finishing overnight and one not finishing at all.
  • The clock stops shrinking. In the approximated dynamics the collective event carries rate u/m instead of m separate rate-u events feeding Γ, so event-driven time increments stay O(1) and the measured time-to-condensation no longer drowns in a flood of microscopic ticks.
  • Statistics become affordable. Measuring T(L) requires averaging over many independent runs. The approximation made ensembles of hundreds of runs feasible at sizes where the exact dynamics could produce only a handful — enough to establish the scaling of T(L) with system size, which was the whole point.

Both effects together are why the interactive demo on this page can show you the symmetric condensate forming in seconds, where the faithful single-particle simulation would keep you waiting.

Why this matters

Condensation in mass-transport models is a paradigm for phase separation in driven, non-equilibrium systems:

  • Granular matter: shaken granular particles cluster through inelastic collisions, producing an effective attractive dynamics of exactly this destination-biased type.
  • Network traffic: packets or vehicles pile up at bottlenecks — condensation at a single "site" of a network.
  • Coagulation / aggregation: the mean-field limit maps to the Smoluchowski coagulation equation, where the explosive regime corresponds to instantaneous gelation.
  • Active matter: self-propelled particles with quorum-sensing recruitment show runaway clustering when local density enhances attraction — the same positive-feedback structure.

The line between normal coarsening (equilibration time diverges) and explosive condensation (it vanishes) is a sharp qualitative boundary, and knowing that the boundary survives the restoration of symmetry tells you it is not an artefact of directed transport, but a robust feature of super-linear positive feedback.

About these demos

Sites are picked uniformly from the non-empty ones; the destination is chosen with probability ∝ mβ (Demo 1: either neighbour; Demo 2: the right neighbour only, with a hop probability normalized per attempt so the animation stays lively). Time is counted in particle-moves. Sites with ≥15 particles are treated as clusters under the large-cluster approximation described above: they can move collectively (the whole mass hops as a unit) or break up (shedding a small shard, ε = 2%). This is the same fast-coarsening approximation I used in the original simulations.
