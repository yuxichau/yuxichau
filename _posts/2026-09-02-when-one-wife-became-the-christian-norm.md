*This is an imagined learning conversation. N. T. Wright and Mary Beard did not have this conversation, and the dialogue below should not be read as a transcript or as a set of quotations from either person.*

## The question

**Mary Beard:** We should begin by refusing the tidy version of the story. People sometimes say that Christianity took a polygamous biblical world and invented monogamy. That gives Christianity too much credit and the ancient world too little complexity.

**N. T. Wright:** Agreed. The Bible contains polygamous households. Abraham has Sarah and Hagar. Jacob has Leah, Rachel, Bilhah, and Zilpah. David and Solomon have several wives. The stories do not present each arrangement as a simple moral ideal, but neither does the Old Testament announce a general ban on polygyny. The Torah regulates the practice and its consequences: [Deuteronomy 17](https://www.sefaria.org/Deuteronomy.17.17?lang=bi) warns Israel's kings against multiplying wives, [Deuteronomy 21](https://www.sefaria.org/Deuteronomy.21.15-17?lang=bi) addresses inheritance when a man has two wives, and [Exodus 21](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.21.10?lang=bi) protects an existing wife's food, clothing, and marital rights.

**Beard:** And Rome matters. Roman law recognised one legal marriage at a time, although Roman sexual and domestic life was hardly a picture of modern equality or exclusive desire. A respectable man could have slaves, freedwomen, or lovers. Legal monogamy did not mean emotional or sexual monogamy.

**Wright:** That distinction is essential for reading the New Testament. When Paul says that an overseer should be the “husband of one wife” in [1 Timothy 3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Timothy%203&version=NRSVUE), we should ask what the phrase is doing in its setting. It may describe marital fidelity, eligibility for leadership, or a household ordered around one marriage. It is a strong piece of evidence for the church's preferred pattern, but it is not a full history of how Christian monogamy emerged.

## Creation and the shape of desire

**Beard:** You will want to take us back to Genesis.

**Wright:** I will, but cautiously. In [Genesis 2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis%202&version=NRSVUE), the man and the woman become one flesh. Jesus cites that passage in [Matthew 19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2019&version=NRSVUE) when responding to a question about divorce. His argument is about the hardness of the human heart and the goodness of God's creation. He directs attention to the beginning, where marriage joins two people in a single embodied life.

That gives Christians a theological grammar for monogamy. It does not erase the polygamous households in Israel's story, and it does not prove that every Israelite marriage followed the Genesis pattern. Scripture often records social practices while exposing their wounds. Sarah and Hagar, Leah and Rachel, David's household, and Solomon's accumulation of wives show rivalry, exclusion, sexual power, and political danger.

**Beard:** A historian might say that these stories also show how marriage was tied to property, inheritance, labour, and alliances. A household was an economic institution. The question “How many spouses should an individual have?” was never only about romantic ideals.

**Wright:** Of course. Israel's Scriptures treat marriage as part of a larger vocation. The family is meant to become a community of blessing, but the biblical stories repeatedly show what happens when people treat others as instruments for producing heirs, securing power, or satisfying desire.

## A Jewish movement in a Roman world

**Beard:** By the first century, Jewish practice was not uniform. Jewish communities lived in Palestine, Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor, and Rome. They did not all inhabit the same legal and cultural environment.

**Wright:** And the Jesus movement began inside that Jewish diversity before it spread through the Roman world. Jesus' teaching on marriage, Paul's advice to Corinth, and the household instructions in the letters are addressed to communities trying to live faithfully in a world where marriage already had public and economic rules.

**Beard:** Which means that Christianity inherited monogamy from more than one direction. Roman civic culture generally recognised one wife. Many Jewish communities also practised monogamy, even though Jewish law did not simply forbid a man from taking more than one wife. Christians were not choosing between one pure Christian option and one pagan alternative.

**Wright:** Yet Christian faith changed the meaning of the pattern. Paul can speak of the church as one body in which Jew and Gentile belong together. He can also use marriage as an image of the covenant between Christ and the church in [Ephesians 5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians%205&version=NRSVUE). The point is not that a husband owns a wife. The point, at least in the theological argument, is a self-giving union shaped by the Messiah's love.

**Beard:** That theological language could challenge household power, though it could also be used to reinforce it. We should not assume that an elevated account of marriage automatically produced equal marriages.

**Wright:** Quite right. Christian teaching on monogamy has often been accompanied by the failure to honour women as full neighbours and fellow disciples. The doctrinal ideal and the lived institution must be held together and judged together.

## From preference to rule

**Beard:** So when did monogamy become a rule rather than a common preference?

**Wright:** Gradually. Christian communities developed expectations about sexual fidelity, divorce, remarriage, and the suitability of clergy. The “one woman man” language in the Pastoral Epistles became part of that conversation. By the second and third centuries, Christian writers increasingly treated a single marriage as the ordinary Christian norm, while also praising celibacy.

**Beard:** The Roman setting helped. An expanding church had to define households, inheritance, sexual conduct, and leadership within an empire whose legal structures were already largely monogamous. A church can baptise a cultural arrangement and give it a new theological meaning at the same time.

**Wright:** That is a useful way to put it. Christian monogamy was neither a simple survival of Roman custom nor a pure deduction from one biblical verse. It emerged from the interaction of Scripture, Jewish moral reasoning, Jesus' teaching, Pauline communities, Roman law, and the church's own pastoral practice.

**Beard:** And later Christian Europe made the rule more coercive. Church courts regulated marriage. States joined in. The monogamous household became a unit of taxation, inheritance, social respectability, and political order. That history is more complicated than saying “Christianity valued faithful love.” It also valued control.

**Wright:** Yes. Christian theology must confess that the church has sometimes protected the institution while neglecting the person. A marriage doctrine that does not defend the vulnerable has missed the moral shape of the gospel.

## What the argument means now

**Beard:** Modern Christians sometimes use monogamy as proof that Christianity is naturally superior to other cultures. That is historically careless. Rome was already broadly monogamous in law. Jewish communities had their own debates. Other societies have organised family life differently, with their own moral arguments.

**Wright:** And the Christian claim should not be “we invented the better household.” The claim is that in the story of creation, Israel, Jesus, and the church, marriage is called toward a form of faithful, mutual, embodied love. Monogamy can serve that calling, but the number one by itself does not create it.

**Beard:** A Roman husband with one legal wife could still exploit enslaved women. A Christian husband with one wife could still dominate her. Counting spouses is easier than examining power.

**Wright:** Which is why the New Testament's household language should be read alongside Jesus welcoming women as disciples, Paul's insistence that the body is not available for exploitation, and his teaching that husband and wife owe one another mutual authority in [1 Corinthians 7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Corinthians%207&version=NRSVUE). The moral centre is faithful reciprocity.

**Beard:** Then the honest conclusion is that Christianity helped make monogamy a powerful social and theological norm, but it did so in a world where monogamy already existed, and it did not always make monogamous households just.

**Wright:** Yes. Christian monogamy is best understood as a historical formation and a theological vocation. The history keeps Christians humble. The vocation keeps the history from becoming mere antiquarianism. A household is faithful when it becomes a place where another person is loved as a neighbour, not possessed as a resource.
