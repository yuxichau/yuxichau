I first met this work in a post on X that made it sound as if models had been trained to predict weights. That stopped me: I had assumed weights only ever came out of an optimizer. [Rikiya Takehi's post](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/), which I enjoyed enormously, is stranger than that. GPT-6 Astra gets raw weights with no list of options and still names the right game for all four networks. It writes game-playing networks from scratch that beat PPO trained for 100 million steps. It lifts Qwen3.5-0.8B from 7.9% to 48.1% on MATH-500 by finding the direction behind its reasoning loops and subtracting it from 0.016% of the weights.

Reading the setups changed how the result looked. Most of the impressive runs are a Python kernel with the tensors loaded and a budget of forward passes, with no gradients and no training loop. Astra reads the weights as data, reasons about what each part computes, writes new numbers, then runs the network to see whether the edit worked. That is a neural network doing brain surgery on itself with the instruments lying around the theatre. I find it genuinely cool, and less magical than the headline, because the model is editing a program it can print.

The obvious next step is a model that updates its own weights while it works, and I spent a while assuming it cannot run. Autoregressive decoding keeps a KV cache, and every entry was computed under the weights you are about to change, so an edit mid-sequence leaves a prefix that no single model produced. Serving stacks make it worse: weights sit in preallocated, fused, sometimes sharded buffers that kernels and captured CUDA graphs treat as constants, and one copy is shared across a batch of other people's requests. Do the edit between turns instead, drop the cache, keep a checkpoint to roll back to, and it starts to look buildable. I want to see someone try.

As a Go player, Experiment 6 is the one I keep returning to. Astra gets 1.5 million weights (243 → 512×6 → 82) and a forward-pass budget, and at 50 passes its hand-written network beats an equally sized PPO network trained on 20 million games. With zero passes it still wins more than half its games against two smaller PPO nets. So it holds some understanding of Go: it writes liberties, group connectivity, atari avoidance and eye-filling into the layers, the way a player would explain the rules to a beginner. Impressive at first, and more plausible the longer I think about it, because 50 passes at 128 rollouts is 6,400 practice games on top of a prior that knows the basics. I would still bet the resulting player is weak. Ladders, ko fights, shape and influence need a precision six hand-set layers will not carry, and beating a 20-million-game PPO baseline on 9x9 is a low bar.

The part I have not settled is what this does to safety work. The paper's headline edit is essentially abliteration: subtract a direction, leave the rest of the model alone. Refusal in chat models has sat along a single direction since [Arditi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717), which is why [uncensored builds](https://yuxichau.com/posts/notes-on-an-uncensored-qwen38/) keep turning up within days of a release. An LLM that can read weights will find that direction faster than a person with a GPU, so post-training a model out of prohibited ideas starts to look like a speed bump. I expected one of the interpretability groups that has done weight surgery for years. It came from MIT CSAIL's retrieval and language-model systems corner. The post went up on 2 November; Simon Willison [wrote it up](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/3/qwen-can-interpret-model-weights/) the next day, and by 5 November another group had already [posted a benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08647) for the capability. Whoever has a good idea has to ship it before a neighbouring lab finishes its related-work section.
