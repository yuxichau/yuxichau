Paul saw the moral disorder of the Roman world as a problem of worship. In [Romans 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%201&version=NRSVUE), he argued that people had exchanged the Creator for created things. Their distorted desires followed from that exchange.

Paul's answer was the one God of Israel, revealed through Jesus the Messiah.

He made this argument as a Jewish theologian who also understood the Greek-speaking world. In Athens, he referred to an altar dedicated "to an unknown god" and quoted Greek poets. He could use familiar language and ideas without surrendering the centre of his message. Greek philosophy gave him points of contact. Jewish Scripture supplied the story. His encounter with the risen Jesus gave him the conclusion.

Philippians 4:8 shows the same approach. Paul tells Christians to dwell on whatever is true, honourable, just, pure, pleasing, commendable, excellent, and worthy of praise. He almost sounds like a Stoic philosopher. Stoicism also taught the discipline of thought and the pursuit of virtue.

Paul gives those ideas a distinctly Christian setting. He is not telling people to achieve peace through detached self-mastery. He tells them to rejoice in the Lord, bring their anxieties to God in prayer, imitate the life they have seen in him, and trust that the God of peace will be with them. The virtues are shaped by worship, prayer, and the presence of God. They belong within the story of the crucified and risen Messiah.

Christianity took shape in an ideologically versatile world. Jewish Scripture, Greek philosophy, Roman power, and local religious traditions all met there. Paul understood that world well enough to speak its language, while challenging its idols.

Our idols have changed shape. Money, sex, status, national identity, career, comfort, technology, and personal autonomy can all become objects of ultimate devotion. They tell us what to desire, what to fear, and what we need in order to feel secure.

We often debate the moral symptoms separately. Paul would ask what they reveal about worship. What has taken the place of God? What do people trust to save them?

The church should learn Paul's method. We need enough cultural knowledge to recognise what is true and good in the world around us, while also identifying the false gods hidden inside its promises. We should speak in language our neighbours understand, but let Scripture and the lordship of Jesus govern what we say.

Paul's example gives us permission to enter the intellectual world of our time, receive what is true, challenge what is idolatrous, and bring the whole conversation back to God.
