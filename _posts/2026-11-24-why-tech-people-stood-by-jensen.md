A few days of reflection after the Jensen Huang fiasco, and what stays with me is how differently two groups reacted to the same man. [Julia Black's Vanity Fair piece](https://www.vanityfair.com/story/jensen-huang-nvidia-blip) reads him as the too-big-to-fail CEO: a resignation on 12 November over revealed affairs, one involving a woman the State Department has linked to Chinese intelligence, indexes sliding, and a company that turned out to have no real succession plan and no D&O insurance. General commentators largely nodded along. Tech practitioners went the other way. A reinstatement petition drew close to 200,000 signatures, including Elon Musk, Travis Kalanick and Garry Tan, employees kept calling for him back, and Nvidia had just posted a record quarter, $100 billion-plus in sales, under his leadership.

I watched [Lex Fridman's interview with Jensen](https://lexfridman.com/jensen-huang) in March and came away more impressed than I expected to be. I never doubted that he knows his business; what got me was how deep the technical understanding runs. Asked why rack-scale co-design matters, he went straight to Amdahl's law: if compute is half your workload and you accelerate it a million-fold, the whole workload only doubles, so networking, memory and switching become the problems you have to design around. On the early CUDA bet he was blunt: install base defines an architecture, everything else is secondary, so Nvidia put CUDA on every GeForce card, ate a 50% cost increase on a consumer product, and watched its market cap fall to about $1.5 billion before clawing its way back. He also argued that inference is thinking, and thinking is harder than reading, which is why he never believed the inference market would be the easy, commoditized end of the stack. It is hard to see how you replace someone who reasons like that.

People who have worked in a meritocracy that actually runs on shipping know, in their bones, what a person like Jensen changes. Your chip either runs faster or it does not, so competence is legible, and everyone in the room can read it. That is why tech people extend a professional respect to good founder CEOs like Jensen or Elon, flaws and all: they are very, very good at the job, and everyone who has shipped anything knows how rare that is. Marc Andreessen has been [arguing much the same via James Burnham](https://x.com/Business_Nerd_/status/2124228003542536218): professional managers can run what already exists, founders are the ones who build what is next. The respect translates much less to businesses where innovation is not the focus, which covers most of the economy, and I think that gap explains the two opposite reactions better than any morality tale.

The pieces against his leadership culture also feel disconnected from the overwhelming support he gets from the people who worked with him. If we assume some of the accusations are true, none of it sounds wildly out of the ordinary for companies of that size and swagger. What I do think got overlooked is different. One question is whether his conduct materially touched American national security, and the State Department link deserves a serious answer. The wider question is that great founder-CEO culture makes one person the weak point of a company, which is genuinely risky from a corporate governance perspective when Nvidia sits at the center of an AI complex worth nearly half the S&P 500. Strong CEOs also plan successions badly, and Jensen is the extreme case: in that same interview he said "I'm famous in saying that I don't believe in succession planning", his alternative being to spread knowledge so relentlessly that his stated hope was to die on the job. That sounded romantic in March; after two weeks of an interim CEO and sliding indexes, it reads very differently.

This is the blind spot of my own tribe. Builders are optimists with a high tolerance for risk; we assume the brilliant person keeps being brilliant, and resilience becomes somebody else's problem. We often do not feel the nervousness of people who see how fragile the whole arrangement actually is, and those people are watching a real variable. But I land on the optimist side anyway. Jensen is back, [the board reinstated him on 23 November](https://www.channelnewsasia.com/business/nvidia-jensen-huang-resignation-return-stocks-6577476), and my gut tells me that was the right call. This guy is just really good at what he does, and you like to see competent people get to keep doing great things.
