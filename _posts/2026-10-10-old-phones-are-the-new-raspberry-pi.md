The SWE-bench Verified horse race quietly ended this year. [Vals archived the leaderboard in September](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench), seven models packed between 95 and 97 percent, because performance has saturated; new releases are no longer scored on it. An agent that can walk into an unfamiliar codebase and resolve almost any real issue can reproduce what most commercial software does, given a spec, and the price has collapsed: DeepSeek V4 Pro resolves those issues at 96.4 percent for ten cents each, and [Epoch has the cost of reaching a fixed capability milestone falling between 9x and 900x per year](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends). Reproducing what other software does used to be the expensive kind of engineering, and it now rents for pocket change.

What I expect to follow is a wave of rooting. The lock on consumer hardware was only ever as strong as the cost of rebuilding what the vendor stack does, and reimplementing those layers meant months of reverse engineering, which is why only hobbyists bothered and vendors could ignore them. Cheap agent labor changes that arithmetic. Unlock the bootloader, flash LineageOS or postmarketOS, and the model writes the drivers and glue that used to make a port a career project. It is the same pressure I wrote about when [agents became the customer](/posts/monetizing-software-when-agents-become-the-customer/): when outsiders can rebuild what you do, your value has to live somewhere other than lock-in.

The hardware side of hobby computing, meanwhile, got more expensive. The reflex answer to any small compute job has for years been a Raspberry Pi, but the 8GB Pi 5 board alone [lists at $175](https://www.canakit.com/raspberry-pi-5-8gb.html) after the memory-price hikes, the starter kit runs about $310, and a monitor pushes the cart toward $470. That buys a machine with no screen, no battery, no camera, and storage on a microSD card, with a hobbyist margin on every part.

A used flagship does that job for less. The phone market refreshes its installed base on roughly an 18-to-24-month cycle, so every year another cohort of perfectly good flagships lands in the secondhand market and pushes older cohorts down a price notch: on [Swappa's September data](https://swappa.com/blog/used-electronics-resale-data), a Galaxy S25 fetches $425, a Galaxy S23 goes for $238, and a Galaxy S22 costs $177, which is the price of the bare Pi board, give or take. For that you get more compute than the Pi 5, UFS storage instead of a microSD card, a screen, a battery, cameras, radios and an NPU, built at a scale the Pi never touches. The catch was always the software, vendor skins, locked bootloaders, months of porting, and that is precisely what an agent does for ten cents an issue.

I think rooted secondhand phones become the default way to put small compute into physical things, and the waste numbers make me happy about that. The [UN's Global E-waste Monitor](https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024) counts 62 million tonnes of e-waste a year, rising five times faster than documented recycling, with the small IT and telecoms bucket, phones included, collected at just 22 percent. Every flagship that gets a second life as a sensor hub or a kiosk is one less new board manufactured and one less device rotting in a drawer. It is rare for the cheapest option to also be the least wasteful, and here they line up. I like my odds on this prediction.
