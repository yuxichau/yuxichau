SWE-bench Verified is finished as a race: [Vals archived the leaderboard in September](https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench), seven models packed between 95 and 97 percent, with DeepSeek V4 Pro resolving real issues at 96.4 percent for ten cents each. An agent that can resolve almost any issue in an unfamiliar codebase can reproduce what most commercial software does, given a spec, and that capability now rents for pocket change. I expect a wave of rooting to follow. The lock on consumer hardware was only ever as strong as the cost of rebuilding the vendor stack, months of reverse engineering that only hobbyists would attempt. Unlock the bootloader, flash LineageOS or postmarketOS, and the model writes the drivers and glue itself.

The hardware side already makes the case. An 8GB Pi 5 board [lists at $175](https://www.canakit.com/raspberry-pi-5-8gb.html), and with case, power supply, card and a monitor the cart runs toward $470 for a screenless machine with storage on a microSD card. A used flagship does that job for less: phones refresh on roughly an 18-to-24-month cycle, so [Swappa's September data](https://swappa.com/blog/used-electronics-resale-data) has a Galaxy S23 at $238 and a Galaxy S22 at $177, the price of the bare Pi board. That buys more compute than the Pi 5, plus a screen, a battery, cameras and an NPU. The catch was always the vendor software, and that is now the ten-cents-an-issue kind of work. I think rooted secondhand phones become the default way to put small compute into physical things, and the [UN's e-waste numbers](https://ewastemonitor.info/the-global-e-waste-monitor-2024) agree: 62 million tonnes discarded a year, phones among the least collected at 22 percent. The cheapest option is also the least wasteful one, so I like my odds on this prediction.
